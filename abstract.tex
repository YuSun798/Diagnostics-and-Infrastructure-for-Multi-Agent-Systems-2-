Foundation model-based multi-agent systems are moving into production, but research on runtime reliability and efficiency remains split between diagnostics and serving infrastructure. This review synthesizes 111 references, 45 systems, and 8 benchmarks across two operational layers: diagnostics (failure localization, observability, safety, and benchmarks) and infrastructure (memory management, prefix-aware scheduling, cache lifecycle, and power management). Rather than treating these literatures independently, we organize the evidence around the runtime state that each layer consumes and manages. We identify three workload characteristics---sparse activation, invocation distance, and inter-agent memory sharing---and show how surveyed systems exploit different subsets of them. Across diagnostics, trace richness and replayability determine what failures can be localized; across infrastructure, memory and latency policies determine what state remains available. Their intersection yields a \emph{diagnostics--infrastructure retention tension}: richer diagnostic evidence favors retaining state, while serving efficiency favors reclaiming it. We use explicit evidence tiers to distinguish full-text-verified quantitative results from abstract-level or contextual evidence, and we avoid cross-system rankings when benchmarks, protocols, or observability settings differ. We conclude with four co-design directions: retention-aware eviction, failure-aware scheduling, budgeted observability, and infrastructure-triggered diagnostics.
